What typewriters taught me about editing

Mira Ashworth·Jul 28, 2026·6 min read·Process

A few years ago I bought a typewriter at a flea market for the price of a decent lunch. I told myself it was for decoration. It sat on a shelf for four months before I fed it a sheet of paper out of boredom, and something about the resistance of the keys — the small mechanical argument every letter has to win before it lands on the page — changed the way I thought about a first draft.

On a laptop, deleting a sentence costs nothing. You can write and unwrite the same paragraph nine times before lunch and never notice the toll it takes, because the interface is designed to make revision invisible. That invisibility feels like freedom. It is also, I've come to think, a quiet tax on your attention — every unwind is a small vote of no confidence in the sentence you just wrote, and you can spend an entire morning voting against yourself.

The tyranny of the backspace key

A typewriter has no memory of what it just did. Once a letter strikes the page, it belongs to the page. That constraint sounds punishing, and for the first few pages it is — you become acutely aware of every clumsy clause, because there is no quiet way to take it back. But somewhere around the fifth page, the panic turns into something closer to discipline. You start a sentence knowing roughly where it ends, because you can no longer afford not to.

Editing on paper isn't nostalgia. It's a way of making the cost of a decision visible again.

None of this is an argument against computers, obviously — I'm typing this on one. It's an argument for noticing which parts of your process have become frictionless, and asking whether that friction was doing more work than you gave it credit for. Word processors didn't just make writing faster. They removed a set of small, constant decisions and replaced them with the option to decide later, indefinitely, which is not the same thing as freedom.

Borrowing constraints on purpose

These days I draft essays in two passes. The first is on the typewriter — no backspace, no formatting, no research tabs open in another window. Just the argument, made once, imperfectly. The second pass is where the computer earns its keep: restructuring, fact-checking, cutting the paragraphs that only existed to help me find the real one.

  • Draft one: constraint on purpose — a single tool, no undo, no research.
  • Draft two: full toolkit — restructure, verify, and cut without mercy.
  • Draft three: read it aloud, once, and fix only what your mouth trips over.

I don't think everyone needs a typewriter. I think everyone needs one stage of their process where taking something back is expensive enough that you stop doing it by reflex. Find whatever object or habit manufactures that friction for you, and protect it the way you'd protect any other tool that's earned its place on the bench.

What actually changed

My sentences got shorter, mostly by accident. I stopped opening essays with three paragraphs of throat-clearing, because throat-clearing on a typewriter costs you a whole sheet of paper. And I started trusting first thoughts more, not because first thoughts are always right, but because the alternative — endless frictionless revision — was quietly training me to trust nothing I wrote until a computer had helped me second-guess it seven times.

The machine on my shelf isn't decoration anymore. It's the part of the process where I find out what I actually think, before I let myself get clever about it.
